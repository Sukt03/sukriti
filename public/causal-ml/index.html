1. The drug that looked bad

Hospital data: 58% recover with the drug, 72% without. So the drug is bad?
Psst. I'm Severity. I sent the really sick people to get the drug.
Split by severity. Mild: 90% vs 80%. Severe: 50% vs 40%. The drug wins by +10 in both!
Adjust for Zed and the truth shows: drug helps by +10 points.

LessonA hidden common cause can flip the answer. Compare within groups.

2. Cole's college

In the whole city, being smart and being sporty are unrelated.
My college lets you in if you're smart OR sporty.
Wait, inside the college, smart students are less sporty?!
That's me. Look only inside a collider and fake links appear.

LessonNever filter or adjust on something both X and Y cause.

3. Coin takes over

I pick who gets treated. My favourites always look great.
Not anymore. Heads: treated. Tails: not treated.
Both groups look the same now. I can't hide in either one!
So a plain difference in averages is the true effect.

LessonRandomise when you can. It removes every confounder at once, even unknown ones.

4. The weighting trick

Old data, no coin. Only 10 of 50 mild patients got the drug.
Chance of treatment for mild = 10/50 = 0.2. So each one counts 1/0.2 = 5 times.
Now both groups have 50 mild and 50 severe. A fair fight!
Balanced again... the weights did Coin's job.

LessonInverse probability weighting turns old, unfair data into a fake randomised trial (if you measured all the Zeds).

The whole idea in 5 lines

Read this first. Everything else is details.

1. A cause means: things would be different if we changed it. For each person there are two stories, "with" and "without", and we only get to see one.

2. Just comparing people who got something with people who didn't can trick you, because they were different to begin with (that's Zed).

3. The best fix is a coin flip (an RCT). Then the two groups start out the same.

4. When you can't flip a coin, you fix it afterwards: compare like with like, give weights (IPW), or combine a guess with weights (AIPW).

5. All of these only work if you measured every Zed. That part needs thinking, not computing.

Why each method was invented

Every method was made to fix a problem with the one before it.
WhenMethodThe problem it fixed
1923Potential outcomes (Neyman)Gave a way to write "what would have happened" for crop experiments
1920s–1935Randomisation (Fisher)Farmers chose which fields got fertiliser. A coin flip stops that choice from fooling you
1952Horvitz–Thompson weightingSurveys reached some people more than others. Weight by 1 / chance of being picked
1974Rubin's potential outcomesMost real data has no coin flip. Rubin brought "two stories per person" to such data (Holland named it the Rubin Causal Model in 1986)
1983Propensity score (Rosenbaum & Rubin)Too many things to match on. Squash them into one number: the chance of being treated
1990sIPW for causal effects (Robins and colleagues)Treatments that change over time (like HIV drugs) broke regression. Weighting handled it
1994AIPW, doubly robust (Robins, Rotnitzky & Zhao)IPW alone is jumpy and relies on one model being right. Adding an outcome model gives two chances to be right
1995–2000DAGs and the backdoor rule (Pearl)People adjusted for the wrong things. Arrow pictures show what to adjust for
2016–2019Meta-learners (Künzel et al.) and DML (Chernozhukov et al.)Lots of data and features. Use machine learning without letting its mistakes bias the answer

The chain: coin flip (best) → can't flip? match like with like → too many things to match? propensity score → weights are jumpy? add a model (AIPW) → too many features? machine learning (DML).

0. The Rubin Causal Model (RCM)

Also called the potential outcomes framework. It defines what "cause" means.
Each person i has Yi(1) and Yi(0)
ITEi = Yi(1) − Yi(0)
ATE = E[ Y(1) − Y(0) ]

Fundamental problem: you only ever see one of the two. Causal inference is a missing-data problem.

Three assumptions let you estimate the ATE from data:

AssumptionPlain wordsBreaks when…
1. SUTVAMy outcome depends only on my own treatmentVaccines: your shot protects me too
2. IgnorabilityGiven x, treatment is as good as randomAn unmeasured Zed exists
3. PositivityEveryone had some chance of both options: 0 < e(x) < 1All severe patients always get the drug
If all 3 hold:
ATE = Σₓ P(x) · ( E[Y | T=1, x] − E[Y | T=0, x] )

Mini numerical (positivity): severe patients: 10 treated, 0 untreated. E[Y | T=0, severe] has no data, so the severe group's effect can't be computed. That's a positivity failure, and no estimator fixes it.

RCM says what the effect is. A DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) tells you which x to adjust for so ignorability holds. Ignorability can't be tested from data; you argue for it.

Every person has two futures: with the drug and without. You only ever see one.

2. Why "treated vs untreated" lies

I made the sicker people (A and C) take the drug. Now your comparison is off.

What you observe: A and C were treated, B and D were not.

Naive = mean(5, 4) − mean(7, 7) = 4.5 − 7 = −2.5

Split it up (same numbers as card 1):

Effect on treated (ATT) = mean(−3, −5) = −4
Selection bias = mean Y(0) of treated − mean Y(0) of untreated
= mean(8, 9) − mean(7, 7) = 8.5 − 7 = +1.5
Naive = ATT + bias = −4 + 1.5 = −2.5 ✓

The treated group was sicker anyway (higher Y(0)), so the drug looks weaker than it is.

3. Fix it: compare within groups

Compare like with like, then average the groups.

Recovery rates. Severe patients mostly got the drug.

GroupDrugNo drugDiff
Mild (50 people)9/10 = 90%32/40 = 80%+10
Severe (50 people)20/40 = 50%4/10 = 40%+10
All mixed29/50 = 58%36/50 = 72%−14 😬
Adjusted = Σ P(group) × diff = 0.5×10 + 0.5×10 = +10 points

Mixed together the drug looks harmful (−14). Within every group it helps (+10). This flip is Simpson's paradox.

4. What to adjust for

Don't condition on me! It creates fake links between X and Y.
Third variable is a…PictureAdjust?

Collider example: a college admits students who are smart or sporty. Inside the college, smart students tend to be less sporty, even though in the population the two are unrelated.

Adjust only for things that happen before treatment and cause it.

6. Overlap (positivity)

If almost everyone like you got treated, there's nobody to compare you with. Hehe.
e = 0.5 → weight 2
e = 0.95 → control weight 1/0.05 = 20
e = 0.99 → control weight 1/0.01 = 100

One person standing in for 100 makes the estimate jump around.

Always plot propensity scores for both groups. No overlap means no trustworthy answer for those people. Clip, trim or change the question.

7. AIPW (doubly robust)

Predict first, then fix the prediction with weighted errors.
ψ = μ₁(x) − μ₀(x) + T·(Y − μ₁)/e − (1−T)·(Y − μ₀)/(1−e)

μ₁, μ₀ = models predicting Y with and without treatment. One treated person with μ₁ = 6, μ₀ = 4, Y = 7, e = 0.5:

ψ = (6 − 4) + (7 − 6)/0.5 = 2 + 2 = 4

ATE = average ψ over everyone.

Correct if either the outcome model or the propensity model is right. Both wrong: still biased.

8. Randomisation

I decide who's treated. Zed can't push anyone, so the groups match.
Coin flip ⇒ mean Y(0) treated = mean Y(0) untreated
⇒ selection bias = 0
⇒ naive difference = ATE

In card 2 the bias was +1.5 because Zed picked who got treated. With a coin, on average it's 0.

This is why A/B tests and RCTs are the gold standard.

9. Effects that differ by person (CATE)

Same experiment, but young and old people react differently.

10. The full recipe

Six steps, in this order. Don't skip step 2.
1. Question: effect of what, on what, for whom?
2. Draw the graph (who causes what)
3. Pick adjustment set (card 4)
4. Check overlap (card 6)
5. Estimate: regression / IPW / AIPW / DML
6. Stress-test: what if there's a hidden Zed?

DML in one line: use ML to predict Y from x and T from x, then regress the leftover part of Y on the leftover part of T (with cross-fitting).

ML helps with step 5 only. Steps 1 to 4 are thinking, not fitting.

Confounding

Z causes both X and Y. Regressing Y on X alone picks up the path through Z. Adding Z to the model closes that backdoor.

Try

  • Set the true effect to 0. The naive slope still shows a strong "effect".
  • Make Z → Y negative: the naive estimate can flip sign (Simpson's paradox).

Heterogeneous effects (CATE: Conditional Average Treatment Effect)

Randomised experiment, but the effect depends on X: τ(x) = base + slope·x. Meta-learners estimate τ(x) with ordinary regressions.

Notes

  • T-learner: fit one model on treated, one on control, subtract.
  • S-learner: one model with T as a feature. A model that cannot express T×X interactions returns a flat effect.
  • Small n + big noise: T-learner slope gets unstable. That is why X-learners, R-learners and DML exist.

N5. Cole's college in numbers

Filter on me and watch a link appear from nothing.

100 students, 25 of each type: smart+sporty, smart only, sporty only, neither. Smart and sporty are independent. The college admits anyone smart or sporty.

Among admitted students, find P(sporty | smart) and P(sporty | not smart).

Show steps
Admitted: smart+sporty 25, smart only 25, sporty only 25
(the 25 "neither" are rejected) → 75 students
P(sporty | smart, admitted) = 25 / (25+25) = 0.5
P(sporty | not smart, admitted) = 25 / 25 = 1.0
In the full 100: P(sporty | smart) = 25/50 = 0.5, P(sporty | not smart) = 25/50 = 0.5

Inside the college: 0.5 vs 1.0. Outside: 0.5 vs 0.5.

No link in the population, a strong negative link among the admitted. That is collider bias.
